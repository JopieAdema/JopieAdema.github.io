<details class="paper">
<summary>
<div>
<div class="paper-title">Statistical Power in Immigration–Crime Studies: A Comment on Riaz (2026)</div>
<div class="paper-outlet"><em>Comment on Riaz (2026), British Journal of Political Science &nbsp;&middot;&nbsp; Rejected by journal</em></div>
</div>
</summary>
<div class="paper-body">
<div class="paper-abstract">A study published in the British Journal of Political Science ("Null by design") arguing that regional immigration–crime studies lack statistical power calibrates its power simulation on residual crime volatility that comes almost entirely from immigration-law offences in a few counties on the Austrian border. Without these offences, which record the act of migration rather than offending by immigrants, the design reaches 80% power at immigrant–native crime rate ratios below those observed for asylum seekers.</div>
</div>
</details>

<details class="paper">
<summary>
<div>
<div class="paper-title">Threat and Assimilation: Evidence from Refugees in Germany: Comment</div>
<div class="paper-outlet"><em>Comment on Jaschke, Sardoschau and Tabellini (2025), Economic Journal &nbsp;&middot;&nbsp; Draft available upon request</em></div>
</div>
</summary>
<div class="paper-body">
<div class="paper-abstract">A study published in the Economic Journal finding that refugees assigned to more hostile German regions converge culturally toward natives faster relies on index components, chiefly risk preferences, that can respond to local hostility for reasons other than assimilation, and clusters standard errors by respondent although the treatment varies across only 38 regions. Dropping risk preferences cuts the estimate by more than half, and with clustering by region the main estimate is no longer significant.</div>
</div>
</details>

<details class="paper">
<summary>
<div>
<div class="paper-title">More People, More Patents? Instruments for Diversity: A Comment on Posch, Schulz and Henrich (2026)</div>
<div class="paper-outlet"><em>Comment on Posch, Schulz and Henrich (2026), Journal of Political Economy &nbsp;&middot;&nbsp; Draft available upon request</em></div>
</div>
</summary>
<div class="paper-body">
<div class="paper-abstract">A study published in the Journal of Political Economy finding that surname diversity causally raises innovation in US counties uses a diversity measure that rises with population growth, while patents are scaled by fixed 1900 population, and an instrument that is almost exactly the logarithm of the number of distinct surnames counted in the same census.</div>
</div>
</details>
